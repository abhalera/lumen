SCIENCE / EYE / THE EYE AS A CAMERA
CONCEPT 01 · 01 / THE HUMAN EYE

Your eye is a camera that focuses light on a screen.

Slide the object closer. Where do the rays meet — and what changes inside the eye?

5 min · NCERT 10.1 · PDF pp. 1–2 · printed p. 161–162

Light enters through the transparent cornea, passes the iris and pupil (which control brightness), then the crystalline lens, and lands on the retina.

Most bending happens at the cornea. The lens only fine-tunes the focus. The retina's light-sensitive cells convert light into electrical signals that travel along the optic nerve to the brain — which is why the image the eye forms is inverted, yet you perceive the world upright.

Camera rule: combined eye optics (cornea + lens, simplified model) focus light → sharp image on retina

Go deeper

An eyeball is about 2.3 cm across. In the sim, the retina sits a fixed 2.3 cm behind the combined lens position (simplified model lumps cornea + crystalline lens at one plane): the combined eye power must supply about 1/0.023 ≈ 43.5 dioptres for a distant object. In reality the cornea supplies most (about two-thirds) and the lens fine-tunes. Iris and pupil handle brightness, not focus — students often confuse the two.

Worked example. Object at 2 m needs combined eye power (cornea + lens, simplified model) 43.48 + 100/200 ≈ 43.98 D. Object at 1 m needs 44.48 D combined. The extra half dioptre comes from the ciliary muscles squeezing the lens thicker — the start of accommodation, the next concept.

SCIENCE / EYE / FOCUS NEAR AND FAR WITH THE SAME EYE
CONCEPT 02 · 01 / THE HUMAN EYE

Hold this page closer… closer… when does it blur — and why?

Bring the slider inside 25 cm. What runs out first?

6 min · NCERT 10.1.1 · PDF pp. 2–3 · printed p. 162

The ciliary muscles change the lens curvature: relaxed for distant objects (thin lens, long focal length), contracted for near ones (thick lens, short focal length). This adjustment is accommodation.

The near point (least distance of distinct vision) is about 25 cm for a young adult; the far point is infinity. The image distance never changes — the retina is fixed — so the combined eye power must (cornea fixed; crystalline lens changes shape to add power in this model). With age the lens stiffens (cataract can cloud it, restored by surgery).

Same retina → combined eye power must change: near needs about +4 D extra (lens adds it; cornea fixed)

Go deeper

You cannot reduce the focal length without limit: below ~25 cm even maximum accommodation leaves the focus behind the retina, so the image blurs and the eye strains. That is E8 of the exercise. Far point at infinity needs the relaxed lens: no effort for distant vision in a normal eye.

Worked example. Near point 25 cm demands extra power 1/0.25 = 4.0 D over distance vision. So the young eye's combined range (simplified model) is roughly 43.5 D (infinity) to 47.5 D (25 cm) — the +4 D amplitude the sim uses.

SCIENCE / EYE / NEAR CLEAR, DISTANT BLURRED
CONCEPT 03 · 02 / DEFECTS OF VISION

Why can your friend read an SMS but not the bus number?

With the slider at 0, where is the focus? Which sign of power pulls it back?

6 min · NCERT 10.2(a) · PDF pp. 3–4 · printed p. 162–163

In myopia (near-sightedness) the eyeball is too long or the lens too curved, so a distant object's image forms in front of the retina. Near objects still focus fine; the far point has moved in from infinity — sometimes to just a metre or two.

A concave (diverging) lens of suitable power spreads the rays before they enter, pushing the focus back onto the retina. Diverging power is negative: a far point of 80 cm needs f = −80 cm, P = −1.25 D.

Myopia: focus in front → correct with concave (minus) lens

Go deeper

Myopia's two causes (excessive lens curvature, elongated eyeball) both add converging power. The concave spectacle lens subtracts exactly that excess for distant vision; for near objects the myopic eye needs less help, which is why reading is clear. Never prescribe convex here — it would add power and worsen the error.

Worked example. In-text Q2: far point 1.2 m → concave lens, P = −1/1.2 ≈ −0.83 D. Exercise Q6: far point 80 cm → concave, P = 100/(−80) = −1.25 D. Sign first, number second.

SCIENCE / EYE / DISTANT CLEAR, NEAR BLURRED — AND OLD-AGE EYES
CONCEPT 04 · 02 / DEFECTS OF VISION

Why does grandfather hold the newspaper at arm's length?

Near object, weak lens: does the focus fall short or overshoot? Which sign fixes it?

6 min · NCERT 10.2(b)–(c) · PDF pp. 4 · printed p. 163–164

In hypermetropia the eyeball is too short or the lens too flat, so near-object rays focus behind the retina. The near point shifts out beyond 25 cm. A convex (converging, plus) lens adds the missing power.

Presbyopia is the age version: ciliary muscles weaken and the lens stiffens, so accommodation fades. Suffering both defects? Bifocal lenses: concave on top for distance, convex below for reading. Contact lenses or surgery are modern options.

Hypermetropia: focus behind → convex (plus) lens; presbyopia: lost accommodation → similar aid

Go deeper

Near point 1 m with normal 25 cm (Exercise Q7): the convex lens must image an object at 25 cm as a virtual image at 1 m: 1/f = 1/(−1.00) − 1/(−0.25)... by 1/v − 1/u = 1/f: −1 + 4 = 3 → P = +3 D. Convex adds power; concave would push focus further behind.

Worked example. Exercise Q5: distance lens −5.5 D → f = 100/(−5.5) ≈ −18.2 cm (myopia part); near lens +1.5 D → f ≈ +66.7 cm. Opposite signs, opposite defects — the classic bifocal pair.

SCIENCE / EYE / A PRISM BENDS LIGHT TOWARD ITS BASE
CONCEPT 05 · 03 / PRISM & DISPERSION

Glass slab keeps rays parallel. What does a triangle do differently?

Trace one colour at i = 50°. Is the emergent ray parallel to the incident ray?

5 min · NCERT 10.3 · PDF pp. 5–6 · printed p. 165

A triangular prism has non-parallel faces meeting at the angle of the prism (A = 60° here). A ray bends toward the normal entering glass, away leaving it — and because the faces tilt, the two bends add instead of cancelling.

The emergent ray tilts from the incident direction by the angle of deviation D (D = i + e − A), bending toward the base. Activity 10.1 pins this down with PE (incident), EF (refracted), FS (emergent), and angles i, r, e.

Prism: emergent ray tilts toward the base by D = i + e − A

Go deeper

Unlike the slab, no lateral-shifted parallel ray survives: D is nonzero for every oblique entry. At small incidence the second face can totally internally reflect — the sim shows this honestly instead of hiding it. Refraction at each face still obeys Snell's law; the prism shape only changes the geometry.

Worked example. Take i = 50°, yellow n = 1.52: r1 = asin(sin50/1.52) ≈ 30.3°, r2 = 60 − 30.3 = 29.7°, e = asin(1.52·sin29.7) ≈ 48.9°, D = 50 + 48.9 − 60 ≈ 38.9°. Check the sim readout — it must agree.

Source: §10.3 + Activity 10.1 · PDF pp. 5–6 · printed p. 165 · jesc110 PDF

SCIENCE / EYE / WHITE LIGHT IS SEVEN COLOURS TRAVELLING TOGETHER
CONCEPT 06 · 03 / PRISM & DISPERSION

Why does one beam become a rainbow band?

Switch to White. Which colour hugs the incident direction, and which strays farthest?

7 min · NCERT 10.4 · PDF pp. 6–7 · printed p. 166–167

Different colours bend by different amounts: red least, violet most (glass slows violet more). The prism fans white light into VIBGYOR — this splitting is dispersion, the band is a spectrum.

Newton proved white is composite: a second inverted prism recombines the spectrum into white. A rainbow is nature's version — droplets refract, internally reflect, then refract sunlight again — always opposite the Sun. Try a fountain with the Sun behind you.

Dispersion: red deviates least, violet most; spectrum recombines to white

Go deeper

Red deviates least because its refractive index is smallest (~1.51 vs ~1.54 for violet) — check the sim's D readouts ordering at any i. Newton could not split a single spectral colour further with a second prism: each is already pure. Rainbow order follows the same deviation ordering, flipped by the internal reflection.

Worked example. From the sim at i = 50°: D_red ≈ 38.1°, D_yellow ≈ 38.9°, D_violet ≈ 40.7°. Small per-face differences accumulate into a visible fan — and the second prism undoes exactly this fan.

SCIENCE / EYE / TWINKLING STARS, BLUE SKIES, RED SUNSETS
CONCEPT 07 · 04 / ATMOSPHERE & LIGHT

Why do stars shiver but planets stare back steadily?

Open Twinkle and flicker the star. Why would a planet refuse to play along?

7 min · NCERT 10.5–10.6 · PDF pp. 7–10 · printed p. 168–170

Atmospheric refraction: starlight bends through air of changing density, so a star's apparent position jitters — it twinkles. Planets are extended discs: many point-flickers average to zero. The same bending shows the Sun ~2 min before sunrise and after sunset, and flattens its disc.

Scattering: fine air molecules scatter short blue light most (NCERT notes red wavelength about 1.8× blue as a rough textbook comparison) → blue sky. Long sunset paths scatter blue away → red Sun. Smoke or mist makes beams visible (Tyndall effect); big particles scatter white. Red, least scattered, marks danger.

Twinkle = unsteady refraction; blue sky + red sunset = selective scattering; red = least scattered

Go deeper

Apparent star position sits slightly above the true one (bent toward the normal). No atmosphere → no scattering → dark sky for astronauts. Danger red is an application of least-scattering, not brightness. Particle size rules colour: fine → blue, larger → longer wavelengths, large → white.

Worked example. Sunrise arithmetic: 2 min early + 2 min late stretches the day by ~4 min versus geometry alone. Wavelength check (visible light only): blue ≈ 470 nm, red ≈ 650 nm → red/blue ≈ 1.38. Both lie inside the visible band (about 400–750 nm); red is scattered least so it survives the long sunset path. NCERT's 'about 1.8×' is a rough comparison — do not multiply it literally (470 nm × 1.8 ≈ 850 nm would land in the infrared, beyond red).

Source: §10.5–10.6 · PDF pp. 7–10 · printed pp. 168–170 · jesc110 PDF
