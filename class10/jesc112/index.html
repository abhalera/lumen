SCIENCE / MAG / MAP THE INVISIBLE: MAGNETIC FIELD LINES
CONCEPT 01 · 01 / FIELD & LINES

How do you draw something you cannot see?

Drag the probe around the magnet. Which way does needle-north always point?

5 min · NCERT 12.1 · PDF pp. 1–3 · printed p. 195–197

A field fills the space where a magnet's force acts. Iron filings (Activity 12.2) and a stepped compass (Activity 12.3) reveal field lines: the path a free north pole would take.

Rules: lines run N → S outside, S → N inside (closed loops); crowded = stronger; never cross — one crossing would give the compass two directions at once. A compass deflects near a magnet because it is a small magnet itself (In-text Q1).

Field lines: closed N→S loops · crowded = strong · never cross

Go deeper

Direction is defined by where a north pole would move — so arrows leave N and enter S outside the magnet. Strength-from-crowding is why the probe reads 'strong' near poles. Inside- magnet direction (S → N) completes each loop; students often draw open lines stopping at S — that breaks the closed-curve rule.

Worked example. Probe walk: at the N pole the needle points away along the line; carried to S it swings to point into the pole. Deflection grows toward either pole — matching Activity 12.3's observation.

SCIENCE / MAG / A WIRE'S CIRCLES: DIRECTION BY THUMB, STRENGTH BY I
CONCEPT 02 · 02 / FIELD OF CURRENT

Flip the battery. Which way do the circles swing?

Set I = +3 A, then −3 A. Predict every arrow before you flip.

7 min · NCERT 12.2–12.2.2 · PDF pp. 3–6 · printed p. 197–199

Current makes concentric circular field lines around a straight wire (Activity 12.5 iron filings). Right-hand thumb rule: grip the wire, thumb along current — fingers curl along the field. Out-of-page current → anticlockwise circles.

Flip the current, flip every arrow (Activity 12.4 compass flips east↔west). Bigger current → stronger field (needle deflects more); farther point → weaker (circles spread). East–west power line (Example 12.1): below it the field points south, above it north.

Circles around wire · RH thumb gives sense · stronger with I, weaker with distance

Go deeper

Viewpoint discipline: 'clockwise from east end, anticlockwise from west end' (NCERT) describes the same circles from opposite ends — students mixing ends get the direction backwards. Reversal symmetry is the exam favourite: Activity 12.4's east↔west flip is the whole rule in one observation.

Worked example. Example 12.1 check: current west (−x), point below (−z): B ∝ (−x̂)×(−ẑ) = −ŷ = south; above: north. Sim agrees: INTO-page current (west in side view) draws clockwise circles — below-wire arrows point south.

Source: §12.2–12.2.2 + Activities 12.4–12.5 · PDF pp. 3–6 · printed pp. 197–199 · jesc112 PDF

Straight-wire field · top view

SIMULATION

SCIENCE / MAG / ONE LOOP: STRAIGHT FIELD AT THE CENTRE
CONCEPT 03 · 02 / FIELD OF CURRENT

How can curved circles add up to straight lines?

Set turns = 1. Predict the centre pattern — then raise turns to 100 and compare strength.

6 min · NCERT 12.2.3 · PDF pp. 6–7 · printed p. 200–201

Bend the wire into a loop: each section's circles reach the centre, and by the right-hand rule every section pushes the same way there. Wide arcs look like straight parallel lines at the centre (Fig. 12.8).

n turns multiply the field n times — same current direction in each turn, fields add. Clockwise current viewed from above drives field downward (into the table); anticlockwise drives it up. Activity 12.6's coil-through-cardboard shows the full pattern.

Loop centre: straight lines · n turns → n× field · sense by right-hand rule

Go deeper

Addition, not magic: at the centre all Biot contributions share direction — this is why 'straight lines at centre' (Exercise Q3a: true) holds. Clockwise-loop question (In-text Q1): thumb rule per section or grip rule both give into-the-page inside, opposite outside. Uniform-field diagram (In-text Q2): parallel equidistant lines with arrows.

Worked example. Turns arithmetic: 1 turn at 3 A vs 100 turns at 3 A → sim meter 3 vs 300 units (air). The ×n rule is why electromagnets use coils, not single loops.

SCIENCE / MAG / A MAGNET YOU CAN SWITCH OFF
CONCEPT 04 · 02 / FIELD OF CURRENT

Same current, air vs iron core — how many times stronger?

Set 100 turns, 3 A, air. Predict the iron factor before tapping the core chip.

6 min · NCERT 12.2.4 · PDF pp. 7–8 · printed p. 201–202

A solenoid — many close turns on a cylinder — fields like a bar magnet: one end N, one S. Inside, lines run parallel and straight: uniform field (same at every interior point — In-text Q3 answer (d)).

Slip soft iron inside and the field leaps hundreds of times: an electromagnet (Fig. 12.11 magnetises a steel rod this way). Cut the current and the magnetism dies — unlike a permanent magnet. Field grows with turns and current; reverse current, swap poles.

Solenoid ≈ bar magnet · inside uniform · iron core ×hundreds · off with the switch

Go deeper

Uniform-inside follows from superposition along the axis — the sim draws it as parallel lines per Fig. 12.10. Iron's factor (~hundreds) is shown as ×200 schematic, stated honestly. Electromagnet = coil + soft-iron core (NOT steel — soft iron loses magnetism when current stops; steel would stay magnetic).

Worked example. Meter check: 100 turns × 3 A air = 300 units; iron = 60000. Doubling turns or current doubles the meter — the NCERT proportionality, live.

SCIENCE / MAG / CURRENT IN A FIELD FEELS A PUSH — FLEMING'S LEFT HAND
CONCEPT 05 · 03 / FORCE ON CURRENT

Current right, field up — which way is the kick?

Set B up, I right (Activity 12.7's geometry). Point your left hand before reading the arrow.

7 min · NCERT 12.3 · PDF pp. 8–11 · printed p. 202–204

A current-carrying rod in a magnet's field is displaced: the field pushes the current (Ampère's idea — Activity 12.7's aluminium rod kicks). Reverse current or field → reverse push. Largest push at 90° between current and field.

Fleming's left-hand rule: forefinger = field, middle finger = current, thumb = force/motion — mutually perpendicular. Current means conventional flow: an electron beam counts opposite its motion (Example 12.2: beam at right angles → force into the page, option (d)). Devices named: motor, generator, loudspeakers, microphones, measuring instruments.

Forefinger B · middle finger I · thumb F · biggest at right angles

Go deeper

Perpendicularity is load-bearing: parallel current feels nothing (sim shows ZERO). Bigger I, stronger magnet, longer rod → bigger kick (In-text Q2). Charged-particle questions reduce to the same cross product with q's sign: proton keeps speed and mass, changes velocity and momentum (In-text Q1 — force bends, never speeds). Alpha particle west-deflected-north → B upward (In-text Q3, option (d)).

Worked example. Activity replay: B up + I right gives force OUT of page in the sim's viewpoint; in Fig. 12.12's photographed viewpoint the same physics reads as the rod kicking left — viewpoint, not contradiction. Electron toggle replays Example 12.2: beam + B at 90° → into page.

SCIENCE / MAG / 220 V AT HOME: LIVE, NEUTRAL, EARTH
CONCEPT 06 · 04 / HOME CIRCUITS

Why don't all appliances die when one switch flips?

Switch on Oven + Geyser on the 5 A circuit. Predict the fuse's fate before reading.

6 min · NCERT 12.4 · PDF pp. 11–12 · printed p. 204–205

Mains: live (red) and neutral (black) at 220 V (AC, 50 Hz), plus green earth wired to a buried plate. Two circuit classes: 15 A for heavy loads (geyser, cooler), 5 A for lights/fans.

Every appliance spans live–neutral in parallel with its own switch: equal 220 V each, independent on/off, one failure changes nothing. Metal bodies bond to earth: leaked current drains away instead of through you. Oven 2 kW on 220 V draws 9.1 A — beyond a 5 A circuit (In-text Q2: expect the fuse to blow).

220 V L–N · parallel appliances · earth guards bodies · 15 A power / 5 A light

Go deeper

Parallel-at-home is the payoff of the whole series/parallel chapter: same V, independent switches, fault isolation. Earth is a low-resistance escape path holding the body at earth potential — In-text Q1's second safety measure alongside the fuse; Exercise Q9's full answer. green = earth always (Exercise Q3b: 'green is live' is false).

Worked example. Board check: Bulb 60 + Fan 75 = 135 W → 0.61 A: 5 A holds. Add Oven + Geyser (4000 W) → 18.8 A: 5 A melts, 15 A melts too — split across circuits, as real homes do.

SCIENCE / MAG / FUSES: THE WIRE THAT DIES TO SAVE THE HOUSE
CONCEPT 07 · 04 / HOME CIRCUITS

What happens in the split second live touches neutral?

Hit 'Simulate short circuit'. Predict the current order — amps or hundreds of amps?

5 min · NCERT 12.4 + §11.7 · PDF pp. 12–13 · printed p. 205–206

A fuse (Joule heating, §11.7) sits in series: rated 1–10 A, it melts when current overshoots, breaking the circuit before wiring or appliances burn. Short circuit: live touches neutral directly — R ≈ 0, current explodes (Exercise Q2: increases heavily).

Overloading: too many appliances on one socket, or a voltage hike, draw past rating — same rescue by the fuse. Prevention: separate power/lighting circuits, rated fuses, no socket stacking (In-text Q3). Fuse + earthing are the two statutory guards (In-text Q1).

Fuse melts on excess I · short = L–N touch, huge I · overload = too much load

Go deeper

Series placement is essential (parallel fuse would never see the fault current — echoes the heating chapter's question). Short vs overload: short is near-zero R accident; overload is legitimate loads summing past rating — both end at the fuse. Exercise Q8's answer is the L–N touch definition.

Worked example. Short demo: ≈400 A schematic vs 0.6 A normal load — three orders up, the fuse has milliseconds. Oven-on-5A replay: 9.09 A vs 5 A rating → melts.

Source: §12.4 + §11.7.1 · PDF pp. 12–13 · printed pp. 205–206 · jesc112 PDF
