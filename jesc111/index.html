SCIENCE / ELEC / WHAT FLOWS IN A WIRE — AND HOW WE COUNT IT
CONCEPT 01 · 01 / CURRENT & CIRCUIT

Why does a torch glow only when the switch closes the loop?

Set V = 0 in the lab. Predict the current — then raise V and watch R1 = 20, R2 = 4 share it.

5 min · NCERT 11.1 + 11.3 · PDF pp. 1–4 · printed p. 171–174

Charge in motion is current: I = Q/t, coulombs per second = ampere. In metal wires electrons drift, but history fixed the conventional direction as positive-to-negative — opposite the electrons. Small currents use mA (10−3 A) and microA (10−6 A).

A closed continuous path is a circuit; a switch opens or closes it. Ammeters join in series (current through them); voltmeters across points in parallel. Draw circuits with NCERT symbols: cell, battery, open/closed key, wire joint, bulb, resistor, rheostat, A, V.

I = Q/t · 1 A = 1 C/s · ammeter in series, voltmeter in parallel

Go deeper

One coulomb holds about 6 × 1018 electrons (1 / 1.6×10−19 ≈ 6.25 × 1018 — In-text Q3). Conventional current vs electron flow is the classic sign trap: inside the wires electrons move toward the positive terminal while 'current' is drawn leaving it. An ammeter must be series — parallel would short the element.

Worked example. Example 11.1: I = 0.5 A for 10 min (600 s) → Q = It = 0.5 × 600 = 300 C. That is 300 × 6.25×1018 ≈ 1.9 × 1021 electrons through the filament.

SCIENCE / ELEC / THE ELECTRICAL PUSH: POTENTIAL DIFFERENCE
CONCEPT 02 · 01 / CURRENT & CIRCUIT

What makes charge move, if gravity plays no role?

In the lab, double V with R fixed. Predict I — then explain who supplies the push.

5 min · NCERT 11.2 · PDF pp. 3–4 · printed p. 173–174

Charges need an electric 'pressure' — potential difference: V = W/Q, work per unit charge, in volts (1 V = 1 J/C). A cell's chemistry creates this PD across its terminals; connected to a wire, it sets charges moving and burns chemical energy to keep them moving.

Measure PD with a voltmeter in parallel across the two points. Saying 'PD is 1 V' means 1 J moves 1 C between those points — and a 6 V battery gifts each coulomb 6 J (In-text Q3).

V = W/Q · 1 V = 1 J/C · battery maintains the PD

Go deeper

Water analogy (NCERT's own): a tank at height drives flow through a level tube; the battery is the tank, PD is the height. Gravity is irrelevant for electrons — only PD along the conductor matters. Voltmeter in series would choke the circuit; ammeter in parallel would short it — the placement rules are safety as well as method.

Worked example. Example 11.2: moving 2 C across 12 V costs W = VQ = 24 J. So doubling V at fixed R doubles I (next concept) — the lab shows it live.

SCIENCE / ELEC / ONE LINE THAT RULES EVERY RESISTOR
CONCEPT 03 · 02 / OHM'S LAW

Double the voltage — does the current exactly double?

Tick NCERT data on. Is the textbook wire ohmic? Read its R off the dots.

7 min · NCERT 11.4 · PDF pp. 5–6 · printed p. 175–176

Ohm's law: at constant temperature, V across a metal wire is directly proportional to I through it — V = IR. The constant R is resistance in ohms (1 Ω = 1 V/A). The V–I graph is a straight line through the origin; its slope is R.

A rheostat (variable resistance) trims current without touching the source. Different components pass different currents at one voltage (Activity 11.2) because resistance — atomic opposition to electron motion — differs. Good conductors: low R; insulators: enormous R.

V = IR · I = V/R · V–I line slope = R (constant temperature only)

Go deeper

Temperature proviso matters: a glowing filament heats and leaves the line — that is why bulbs are non-ohmic in use. From Eq. 11.7, doubling R halves I at fixed V — the fastest check in the chapter. Slope reading: R = V/I at any point on the line, including the NCERT dots (mean ≈ 3.3 Ω).

Worked example. Example 11.3: bulb 220 V/1200 Ω → 0.183 A; heater 220 V/100 Ω → 2.2 A — same mains, twelve times the current. Example 11.4: 60 V drives 4 A → R = 15 Ω → at 120 V, I = 8 A.

SCIENCE / ELEC / LONG, THIN, NICHROME: WHAT MAKES R BIG
CONCEPT 04 · 02 / OHM'S LAW

Why do heater coils glow but copper mains stay cool?

Pick copper, then nichrome at the same size. Predict the R ratio before reading it.

6 min · NCERT 11.5 · PDF pp. 7–10 · printed p. 177–180

R = ρl/A: longer wire → more R; thicker wire → less R; material sets resistivity ρ (Ω·m). Copper (~1.6×10−8) vs nichrome (100×10−6): ratio (100×10−6)/(1.6×10−8) = 6,250 — about six thousand times apart — same shape, wildly different R. Table values are NCERT Table 11.2 at 20°C; only the ratio wording is corrected.

Alloys (high ρ, resist oxidation) serve heaters and irons; tungsten's extreme melting point serves filaments; copper/aluminium carry transmission lines. Thick wire passes more current at one voltage (In-text Q2: choose thick). Table 11.2 values are tools, not memory work.

R = ρl/A · thick beats thin · alloy beats pure metal for heaters

Go deeper

Area uses diameter squared: doubling d quarters R (Exercise Q6: 10 Ω → 2.5 Ω). Length scaling is linear (Exercise: l/2 and 2A → R/4 = 1 Ω from 4 Ω). Resistivity ranking answers 'better conductor' instantly: lower ρ wins — silver best, iron beats mercury among the asked pair.

Worked example. Example 11.5: R = 26 Ω, l = 1 m, d = 0.3 mm → A = π(3×10−4)²/4 ≈ 7.07×10−8 m² → ρ = 26×7.07×10−8 ≈ 1.84×10−6 Ω·m → manganese. Example 11.6: R₂ = (1/4)×4 = 1 Ω.

CONCEPT 05 · 03 / COMBINATIONS

Why does one dead fairy-light bulb kill the whole string?

Move the ammeter idea anywhere in the sim loop. Predict: same I or different?

6 min · NCERT 11.6.1 · PDF pp. 11–14 · printed p. 181–184

End to end, one path: the same current I crosses every resistor (Activity 11.4 — ammeter reads equal anywhere). The total PD splits: V = V1 + V2 + V3, each Vi = IRi.

One equivalent resistor replaces all: V = IRs gives Rs = R1 + R2 + R3 — always bigger than any member. Series suits equal-current needs and fails all-if-one-fails: the fairy-light curse.

Series: same I · V adds · Rs = R1 + R2 + …

Go deeper

Derivation is substitution, not magic: IRs = IR1 + IR2 + IR3, cancel I. Equivalent means same V and same I at the terminals — inside, the split differs. Example 11.7 checks every step: 20 + 4 = 24 Ω, 6/24 = 0.25 A, drops 5 V + 1 V = 6 V. Set R1 = 20, R2 = 4, V = 6 in the lab to replay it.

Worked example. Replay of Example 11.7 in the sim: Rs = 24 Ω, I = 0.25 A (250 mA), V1 = 5 V, V2 = 1 V. A single 24 Ω resistor would draw identically — that is what 'equivalent' promises.

SCIENCE / ELEC / MANY ROADS: RESISTORS IN PARALLEL
CONCEPT 06 · 03 / COMBINATIONS

Why does your fan survive the bulb fusing?

Flip to Parallel with R1 = 5, R2 = 10, R3… wait, only two slots — predict I1 vs I2, then read.

7 min · NCERT 11.6.2 · PDF pp. 15–17 · printed p. 185–187

Across the same two points: every branch sees the full V; currents split, I = I1 + I2 + I3, each Ii = V/Ri. Reciprocals add: 1/Rp = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + … — equivalent always smaller than the smallest member.

Homes wire in parallel: equal PD per appliance, independent switches, one failure changes nothing. Series would force one current on all and kill everything on one fault (In-text Q3). Judge at a glance: 1 Ω ‖ 106 Ω ≈ 1 Ω — the small resistor dominates.

Parallel: same V · I splits · 1/Rp sums reciprocals

Go deeper

Example 11.9: (10‖40) = 8 Ω in series with (30‖20‖60) = 10 Ω → 18 Ω, 12/18 ≈ 0.67 A. Three 6 Ω in parallel: 1/6+1/6+1/6 = 1/2 → 2 Ω… and Ex11(ii)'s 4 Ω needs (6+6)‖6.

SCIENCE / ELEC / CURRENT MAKES HEAT — FRIEND AND FOE
CONCEPT 07 · 04 / HEATING EFFECT

Why does the element glow while the cord stays cool?

Set I = 5, R = 20, t = 30. Predict H — then explain the cord with the same I.

6 min · NCERT 11.7–11.7.1 · PDF pp. 18–20 · printed p. 188–190

Energy VQ spent in time t becomes heat H = VIt = I²Rt (Joule's law): square of current, times R, times time. At fixed voltage use V²t/R after I = V/R.

Cord (copper, tiny R) barely warms; element (nichrome, big R) glows — same I, different R. Bulbs exploit it (tungsten, 3380°C melt, nitrogen–argon fill); fuses exploit it for safety: excess current melts a rated wire (1–10 A) in series, breaking the circuit. Iron at 1 kW/220 V draws 4.54 A → fit a 5 A fuse.

H = VIt = I²Rt · high-R glows, low-R cord doesn't · fuse melts on excess I

Go deeper

In-text checks: 96000 C across 50 V in 1 h → H = VQ = 4.8 MJ (time given is a distractor — Q already includes it). Iron 20 Ω, 5 A, 30 s → 25×20×30 = 15000 J. Example 11.10: 840 W → 3.82 A, 57.6 Ω; 360 W → 1.64 A, 134.15 Ω. Example 11.11: 100 J/s in 4 Ω → I = 5 A → V = 20 V. Tungsten/alloy/fuse reasoning is Exercise 18(a)(b).

Worked example. Default sim state replays the in-text iron: 5 A, 20 Ω, 30 s → H = 15000 J. Fuse panel replays the 1 kW iron: 4.55 A holds on 5 A, blows on 3 A.

SCIENCE / ELEC / WATTS, UNITS, AND THE MONTHLY BILL
CONCEPT 08 · 05 / ELECTRIC POWER

Who uses more: a 250 W TV for an hour, or a 1200 W toaster for 10 minutes?

Set Fridge 400 W, 8 h, 30 days, ₹3. Predict the bill — then test TV-vs-toaster.

6 min · NCERT 11.8 · PDF pp. 21–22 · printed p. 191–192

Power is energy rate: P = VI = I²R = V²/R, in watts (1 W = 1 V×1 A; kW = 1000 W). Energy E = Pt; the commercial unit is 1 kWh = 1000 W × 3600 s = 3.6×106 J.

Bill = kWh × tariff. Refrigerator 400 W × 8 h × 30 d = 96 kWh → ₹288 at ₹3 (Example 11.13). Bulb 220 V×0.5 A = 110 W (Ex 11.12). Motor 5 A×220 V = 1100 W; 2 h → 2.2 kWh. Electrons are never 'used up' — you pay for energy, not particles.

P = VI = I²R = V²/R · 1 kWh = 3.6 MJ · bill = units × rate

Go deeper

TV vs toaster (Exercise Q16): 250×1 = 250 Wh beats 1200×(1/6) = 200 Wh — power misleads, energy decides. Bulb on 110 V (Exercise Q3): R = 220²/100 = 484 Ω fixed → P = 110²/484 = 25 W — quarter voltage, quarter power (V²!). Series-vs-parallel heat (Q4): same V → H ∝ 1/R → 1:4. 'Which is not power': IR² (Q2). Lamps on 220 V/5 A (Q12): 10 W each → 110 lamps.

Worked example. Default sim state replays Example 11.13 exactly: 96 kWh, ₹288. TV-vs-toaster presets give 250 Wh vs 200 Wh.

PRACTICE

Check yourself at three levels

Which does NOT represent electrical power?

Check units: power = V×A.

Solution

IR² gives V·Ω — not watts. All others equal VI.

NCERT Exercise Q2

220 V bulb, current 0.5 A. Power?

watt (W)

P = VI.

Solution

220 × 0.5 = 110 W.

NCERT Example 11.12

100 W (220 V) bulb on 110 V consumes…

R fixed; P = V²/R.

Solution

Half voltage → quarter power: 25 W.

KEEP THE BIG PICTURE

Your chapter, on one page.

Current

I = Q/t

Ampere; conventional + to −; ammeter series.
Potential

V = W/Q

Volt = J/C; battery sustains; voltmeter parallel.
Ohm

V = IR

V–I slope = R at constant T; rheostat trims I.
Resistance

R = ρl/A

Long/thin/high-ρ → big R; alloys heat, Cu/Al carry.
Series

Rs = ΣR

Same I, V splits; one break kills all.
Parallel

1/Rp = Σ1/R

Same V, I splits; Rp below smallest; homes.
Heat

H = I²Rt

Joule; fuse = rated weak link in series.
Power

P = VI · 1 kWh = 3.6 MJ

Bill = units × tariff; V² scaling.

Conversions: 1 mA = 10−3 A · 1 μA = 10−6 A · 1 kW = 1000 W · 1 C ≈ 6 × 1018 electrons. Electrons are not consumed — energy is.
